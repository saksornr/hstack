# One-page proposal abstract writing

Produce a self-contained academic abstract that lets a reviewer understand what is unknown, what the study will do, how the evidence will answer the question, and why the answer matters. Preserve the author's research intent and evidentiary status.

## Establish the writing contract

Read the supplied sources before drafting. Extract the research question, specific gap, proposed contribution, design, data/tasks, comparisons, outcomes, analysis, feasibility constraints, and any actual results. Distinguish supplied facts, plans, preliminary observations, and missing information internally.

Follow the user's venue template, audience, word limit, citation style, and page rules. If unspecified, use a descriptive title, optional 3–5 keywords, 3–4 connected paragraphs, and a compact References section. Aim initially for 300–400 body words when references share the page; this is a drafting convention, not a guarantee of one-page fit. Use a shorter body when references must share the page. Do not require headings, keywords, a budget, or a figure unless useful or requested.

If essential information is missing, ask only for what prevents a meaningful draft. Otherwise proceed using supported details and list unresolved items briefly outside the abstract. Do not silently invent a dataset, sample size, model version, experiment, result, budget, or citation. Label suggested design additions as proposals until the user adopts them.

## Build the argument

Use this structure flexibly; do not make every abstract sound identical:

1. **Problem and gap:** State the concrete scientific or practical issue. Identify what existing work or current evidence leaves unresolved. Narrow to an answerable question. Avoid a long history of the field.
2. **Approach and aims:** State what the study proposes and explain the mechanism or design that addresses the gap. Integrate two or three aims only if they clarify distinct questions; avoid redundant numbered promises.
3. **Evaluation:** Identify the data or tasks, comparator/control, primary outcome, and analysis needed to interpret the study. Include methodological details that change credibility, such as matching, blinding, uncertainty, or a key ablation. Do not add these as accomplished facts when absent from the source.
4. **Contribution and scope:** Explain what knowledge or usable evidence the study is expected to provide. State the most consequential scope limitation. Include feasibility, budget, or ethics in proportion to their relevance.

As a starting allocation, give about one quarter of the body to the problem/gap, one third to the approach, one third to evaluation, and the remainder to contribution. Adjust to the study; these proportions are editorial aids, not acceptance rules.

## Keep claims defensible

- Use “we propose,” “we will compare,” and “the study aims to” for unperformed work. Describe completed results only when supplied evidence supports them. Label preliminary findings explicitly and keep their actual scope.
- Separate a hypothesis from a finding and expected contribution from guaranteed improvement. Do not end a proposal with invented positive results to imitate a completed-paper abstract.
- Match causal language to the design. Observed differences across providers do not reveal their hidden moderation mechanisms. A change caused by persona wording in an experiment does not establish innate differences between people.
- Keep treatment, unit of analysis, and outcome aligned. Paired scenarios require paired comparisons; repeated generations do not automatically constitute independent scenarios.
- Define denominators when ambiguity changes interpretation. Separate policy refusal, technical failure, and evaluable response; avoid counting all three as the same event.
- Preserve uncertainty about candidate models, access, versions, prices, and source references. Verify externally when requested or needed to assert current facts. If verification is unavailable, qualify or omit the detail and flag it outside the abstract. Supplied references are not automatically independently verified.
- Use only citations that support the adjacent claim. Never invent bibliographic details or claim a venue requirement from an example's appearance.

## Include citations and references

Include in-text citations and a matching **References** section by default. Follow explicit user or venue instructions if citations are prohibited or a separate reference page is allowed. Keep the whole deliverable to one page, including references, unless the specified rules exclude references from the limit.

Select a small set of directly relevant sources, typically 2–4: the closest prior work establishing the gap and the dataset, benchmark, or method on which the study depends. Treat that range as a space guide, not a quota. Do not add unrelated sources to reach a number. Cite background and borrowed methods; the author's proposed design does not need an invented supporting citation.

Use the requested citation style; otherwise use consistent author–year citations with compact bibliographic entries. Each in-text citation must have one matching entry, and each entry must be cited. Include authors, year, title, venue or repository when supplied, and a DOI or stable URL when available. Do not manufacture missing details or replace a known full author list with invented names.

Use references supplied by the user as supplied, without implying independent verification. Check the original source when verifying a claim or correcting bibliographic metadata; if verification is unavailable, preserve the supplied entry and flag its status outside the submission text. If no usable sources are available, draft the supported content and identify the missing references outside the abstract rather than inserting fictional citations. Add verified sources when research is authorized and available.

Use the cited examples in [worked-examples.md](references/worked-examples.md) as formatting models. Their bibliographic entries come from the supplied documents and require checking before submission.

## Edit for one page

Remove repeated motivation, broad claims of importance, literature catalogues, promotional adjectives, and implementation inventories before cutting the research question or evaluation. Prefer precise verbs and concrete nouns. Define only acronyms used again. Keep a single term for each condition or construct.

For text-only delivery, report body word count if helpful and say page fit is estimated. For DOCX/PDF delivery, use available document tools, apply the venue layout, render the final artifact, and inspect page count and legibility. If no template exists, start with readable 11–12 pt text and ordinary margins; shorten content before shrinking type. Do not claim verified one-page fit from a word count alone. Count title, keywords, figures, and references toward the page whenever the venue does.

## Deliver and check

Return the polished abstract first. Keep editorial notes, unresolved facts, and word-count information outside the submission text. Do not append a critique when the user requests only the final abstract.

Before delivery, check:

- Can a reviewer identify the gap, question, intervention/approach, comparison, primary outcome, and contribution?
- Does each major aim have a corresponding evaluation?
- Are plans and observations clearly separated, with no fabricated findings?
- Are sample size, budget arithmetic, model status, and citations internally consistent or explicitly unresolved?
- Does the contribution stay within the tested population, tasks, models, and conditions?
- Does every in-text citation match a References entry, with no uncited entries or invented bibliographic details?
- Is the requested length satisfied, with actual page fit checked when delivering a formatted file?
